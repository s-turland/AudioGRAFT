Abstract

Audio inversion, or sound matching, is the task of finding the settings of an audio system that best reproduce a target sound. Current techniques assume systems have a broadly fixed structure, which limits the range of sounds it can match. This work instead approaches the task as a graph problem, generating a system’s structure and settings jointly as an arbitrary composition of modular processor types. We propose GRAFT, a two-stage audio inversion process. We first define an autoencoder that maps graphs to a set of node embeddings, aligned according to the sound each graph produces. A flow-based generative model is then trained to produce new graphs that approximate a target audio sample. Across graphs of up to 40 modules, GRAFT produces a valid system for 99–100% of targets, whereas prior tokenised flow-based methods fail to reliably generate valid modular systems for larger graph sizes. The proposed alignment strategy further improves audio reconstruction over graphs of up to 20 connected modules. These findings pave the way for further topological learning across a variety of audio processing systems.

Index Terms— inverse problems, graph generative models, flow matching, representation learning, audio synthesis

1. Method

The method consists of two training stages. Stage 1 is a permutation-equivariant autoencoder that optimises an audio-graph representation with sufficient graph reconstruction, while Stage 2 trains a flow-based velocity model over the pre-trained distribution of latent audio-graph representations (Fig. 1).

2. Audio examples

Generated systems are presented for each maximum graph size, nmax ∈ {10, 20, 40}, and evaluation domain. In-domain conditions are renders of held-out graphs from the Torchsynth data distribution. Out-of-domain conditions are musical instrument (NSynth) and environmental (ESC-50) sound samples, for which no reference graph exists.

Each column contains the render of the graph generated by one method for the given condition. For every method, the graph shown is the best of four samples by MSS distance to the condition, whereas Table 1 reports single samples. The MSS distance is given beneath each render, with the lowest in each row in bold. Generated graphs that do not produce audio are marked as not rendered. Conditions on the first page were selected by the authors; subsequent pages are drawn at random from the remaining test conditions. In-domain examples are currently available for nmax = 10 only. All clips are 4 s, mono and sampled at 16 kHz, without loudness normalisation.

3. Intermediate flow states

For a single condition, the state Zt of the conditional flow is decoded by the Stage 1 decoder and rendered at six times t along the sampling trajectory, from Gaussian noise at t = 0 to the generated graph at t = 1. States early in the trajectory may decode to graphs that do not render. The STFT distance to the condition is given beneath each render.

4. Results

To the best of our knowledge, no previous work models modular audio graph topologies generatively. GRAFT is therefore compared against flow baselines FM-Param2Tok, FM-Node and FM-Flat, which share the same interpolant but differ in tokenisation and symmetry bias. FM-Param2Tok tokenises each parameter and edge as a set element. FM-Node adds per-node positional encodings, and FM-Flat demonstrates graph flow matching interpreted as a single vector with no tokenised symmetry projection. GRAFT-CQT and GRAFT-STFT differ in the audio descriptor used for alignment, while GRAFT-Unaligned omits the alignment objective.

Audio accuracy is assessed with four paired distances between the rendered generated graph and the conditioning signal: multi-scale spectral (MSS), spectral optimal transport (SOT), warped MFCC distance (wMFCC) and the cosine distance between RMS envelopes. Distances are averaged over rendered graphs, with render rate reported separately. Node- and edge-count MAE, exact topological recovery and node-type F1 are also reported where ground truth graphs are known.

Each method performs adequately over small graphs, with a notable increase in audio and graph resynthesis for the proposed methods. GRAFT methods render 99.4–100% of conditions, whereas baseline methods become unstable as graphs increase in size. FM-Flat and FM-Param2Tok render under 18% of in-domain conditions at nmax = 20, and at nmax = 40 FM-Node renders 12.8%, and 0% for FM-Param2Tok. Each GRAFT method attains the lowest spectral error (MSS) at every size and in every domain, and at nmax = 10 recovers the exact graph for 42.1% of conditions, against at most 16.8% for the baseline methods.

For nmax ∈ {10, 20}, both aligned variants outperform GRAFT-Unaligned on each audio distance, in-domain and on NSynth data, reducing error by 18–19% on average at nmax = 10 and 9–11% at nmax = 20. GRAFT-CQT is strongest in all in-domain graphs, while GRAFT-STFT performs the best on both NSynth and ESC-50 at nmax = 10. This is consistent with the CQT’s resolution of harmonic structure suiting pitched material. Alignment also improves graph recovery: at nmax = 10, GRAFT-CQT lowers node- and edge-count MAE by 15% and 13%. At nmax = 40 the alignment advantage diminishes, and GRAFT-Unaligned is best or tied on the majority of reconstruction metrics. This indicates a trade-off between audio alignment and accurate large-graph reconstruction.

Fig. 2 presents the CKA score on the node-set embedding mean, z̄. Aligned GRAFT variants score 0.39–0.53 at every size against 0.13–0.21 for GRAFT-Unaligned, at the cost of reduced MSS reconstruction accuracy at the VAE stage (Fig. 2(a)). The generative audio reconstruction over nmax = 10 and nmax = 20 improves after alignment, indicating that organising latent geometry according to audio symmetries aids downstream generation.
